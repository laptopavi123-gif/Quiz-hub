Geography Explanations

TYPE-I: Solar System & Astronomy

1. Jupiter
Jupiter's radius is about 1/10 the radius of the Sun and its mass is 0.001 times the mass of the Sun. Jupiter's mass is 2.5 times that of all the other planets in the Solar System combined. This is so massive that its barycenter with the Sun lies above the Sun's surface at 1.068 solar radii from the Sun's center.
2. Planets without Moons
Of the nine planets (historically), only Venus and Mercury have no known natural satellites.
3. Moons
A total of 139 moons, or natural satellites, can be found in the vast solar system.
4. Planet Sizes
The planets of the Solar System (by decreasing size) are Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune, Earth, Venus, Mars, and Mercury.
  • Jupiter (69,911 km): 1,120% the size of Earth.
  • Saturn (58,232 km): 945% the size of Earth.
  • Uranus (25,362 km): 400% the size of Earth.
  • Neptune (24,622 km): 388% the size of Earth.
  • Earth (6,371 km).
  • Venus (6,052 km): 95% the size of Earth.
  • Mars (3,390 km): 53% the size of Earth.
  • Mercury (2,440 km): 38% the size of Earth.
4. Orion
The Orion constellation has 81 visible stars, including some of the brightest and most important stars in the night sky. The brightest star in the constellation is Rigel, which is 40,000 times brighter than the sun and emits 100,000 times the energy.
5. Proxima Centauri
Proxima Centauri is the nearest star to the Earth. It is a red dwarf, a small low-mass star, about 4.25 light years from the Sun in the constellation of Centaurus. Because of Proxima Centauri's proximity to Earth, its angular diameter can be measured directly; it is about one-seventh the diameter of the Sun.
6. Blood Moon
A total lunar eclipse is sometimes called a Blood Moon because of the reddish tinge the Full Moon takes on when fully eclipsed. A "blood moon" happens when Earth's moon is in a total lunar eclipse. While it has no special astronomical significance, the view in the sky is striking as the usually whitish moon becomes red or ruddy-brown.
7. Orion (Hunter)
Orion, a prominent constellation located on the celestial equator and visible throughout the world, is also known as the Hunter. It is named after Orion, a hunter in Greek mythology. It is one of the most conspicuous and recognizable constellations in the night sky.
8. Hydra
Hydra is the largest of the 88 modern constellations, measuring 1303 square degrees, and also the longest at over 100 degrees. It is a long, slender winding region of stars named after the many-headed sea serpent Hercules encountered in Greek mythology. Its southern end abuts Libra and Centaurus and its northern end borders Cancer. It is located in the southern hemisphere.
9. The Sun
The Sun is 864,400 miles (1,391,000 kilometers) across. This is about 109 times the diameter of Earth.
10. Neptune
Since the demotion of Pluto to a dwarf planet in 2006, Neptune is now the farthest major planet from the Sun. At 4.5 billion km (2.8 billion miles) from the Sun, it orbits at 5.45 km (3.38 miles) per second, taking 164.79 years to complete one orbit.
11. Ursa Major (Saptarishi)
In Hindu legend, the brightest stars of Ursa Major represent the Seven Sages and the constellation is known as Saptarishi. The sages in question are Bhrigu, Atri, Angirasa, Vasishta, Pulastya, Pulalaha, and Kratu. Ursa Major constellation lies in the northern sky. Its name means "the great bear," or "the larger bear" in Latin.
12. Aurora
Aurora Borealis, sometimes referred to as polar lights, northern lights, or southern lights, is a natural light display in the Earth's sky, predominantly seen in the high-latitude regions. The incredible light show is caused by collisions between electrically charged particles released from the sun that enter the earth's atmosphere and collide with gases such as oxygen and nitrogen. The lights are seen around the magnetic poles of the northern and southern hemispheres.
13. Europa
Europa is the smallest of the Galilean moons of Jupiter, and the sixth closest natural satellite to the planet. Galileo Galilei discovered it along with Io, Ganymede, and Callisto. Despite it being the smallest of the Galilean moons, it is still one of the largest moons in our Solar System.
14. Nebula
A nebula is a giant cloud of dust and gas in space. Some nebulae (plural of nebula) come from the gas and dust thrown out by the explosion of a dying star, such as a supernova. Nebulae exist in the space between the stars—also known as interstellar space. The closest known nebula to Earth is called the Helix Nebula.
15. Terrestrial vs Jovian Planets
Mercury, Venus, and Mars have physical properties similar to Earth and are all referred to as terrestrial planets. The word terrestrial comes from the Latin word terra meaning land. On the other end of the spectrum are the gas giants, known as the Jovian planets: Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune. They are labeled Jovian after Jupiter, the largest of the group. The word Jovian comes from Jove, another name for the Roman god Jupiter.
16. Celestial Object
A natural celestial object is visible in the sky such as a planet, natural satellite, asteroid, comet, or the Moon.
17. Venus (Lucifer)
Lucifer is a Latin name for the planet Venus. Venus is the second planet from the Sun, and the second-brightest natural object in the night sky after the Moon.
18. Lunar Exosphere
Planetary scientists prefer to call the thin gaseous envelope around the Moon as the lunar exosphere. It is so tenuous that the gas atoms very rarely collide with each other. According to ISRO, Argon-40 (40Ar) originates from the radioactive disintegration of Potassium-40 (40K).
19. Order of Planets
The order of the planets in the solar system, starting nearest the sun and working outward is the following: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune. The inner four planets closest to the sun—Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars—are often called the terrestrial planets. The four large outer worlds—Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune—are sometimes called the Jovian or Jupiter-like planets.
20. Venus Rotation
Venus is the second planet from the Sun and the sixth-largest. Together with Mercury, they are the only planets without a satellite. Venus is the hottest planet. Venus has a retrograde rotation, moving in the opposite direction than most planets; only Uranus also does this. They both move from East to West.
21. The Moon
The radius of the Moon is about one-fourth that of Earth and acceleration due to gravity on the Moon is about one-sixth that on the Earth. The radius of the Moon is 1.74 × 106 m. The mass of the moon is 7.35 × 1022 kg.
22. Aphelion and Perihelion
In astronomy, Aphelion refers to the point in the orbit of a planet or comet most distant from the sun. When the Earth is at its aphelion in early July, it is about 4,800,000 km farther from the sun than when at its perihelion in early January. The Earth is moving about our sun in a very nearly circular orbit. It covers this route at a speed of nearly 30 kilometers per second, or 67,000 miles per hour.
23. New Moon
In astronomy, the new moon is the first phase, when the Moon and Sun have the same longitude. In lunar calendars, a lunar month is the time between two successive similar phases (new moons or full moons). The average length of a synodic month is 29.530588 days (or 29 days, 12 hours, 44 minutes, and 2.8 seconds). The stars, the planets, the moon and many other objects in the sky are called celestial objects. The study of celestial objects and associated phenomena is called astronomy.
24. Moons of Jupiter and Saturn
Saturn overtook Jupiter as the planet with most moons in October 2019. Saturn has 274 and Jupiter has 95 moons. (Note: These numbers change with new discoveries).
25. Kepler's Laws
Johannes Kepler published three laws of planetary motion between 1609 and 1619, describing the motions of the planets in the solar system. The laws are: 1. All planets move about the Sun in elliptical orbits, having the Sun as one of the foci. 2. A radius vector joining any planet to the Sun sweeps out equal areas in equal lengths of time. 3. The squares of the periods of revolution of the planets are directly proportional to the cubes of their mean distances from the Sun.
26. Gnomon
A gnomon is the triangular blade of a sundial that casts a shadow. Sundials tell the time relative to the true local moment; that is why the gnomon is in the center of the dial and why it points south. It is lined up with the meridian.
27. Terrestrial Planets
A terrestrial planet, telluric planet, or rocky planet is a planet that is composed primarily of silicate rocks or metals. Within the Solar System, the terrestrial planets accepted by the IAU are the inner planets closest to the Sun: Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars.
28. Galilean Moons
On 7 January 1610, Italian Astronomer Galileo Galilei discovered, using a homemade telescope, three moons orbiting the planet Jupiter. One of those moons was Ganymede, the largest moon of Jupiter. Its name was soon suggested by astronomer Simon Marius, after the mythological Ganymede.
29. Hubble's Law
In 1929, Edwin Hubble provided the first observational evidence for the universe having a finite age. Using the largest telescope of the time, he discovered that the more distant a galaxy is from us, the faster it appears to be receding into space. This means that the universe is expanding uniformly in all directions.
35. Ceres
Dwarf planet Ceres is the largest object in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter. It is the only dwarf planet located in the inner solar system. It was the first member of the asteroid belt to be discovered when Giuseppe Piazzi spotted it in 1801.
36. Centripetal Force
The Moon moves around the Earth in its near-circular orbit due to Centripetal force. Gravitational force exerted by the earth on the moon provides the necessary centripetal force required for the moon to orbit the earth. Centripetal force is an external force that always acts on the body to maintain the circular motion and it is a force towards the center of the path.
37. Geostationary Satellite
A geostationary satellite is an earth-orbiting satellite, placed at an altitude of approximately 35,800 kilometers directly over the equator that revolves in the same direction the earth rotates (west to east). The orbital time period of the geostationary orbit is equal to twenty-four hours or one Earth day. This position allows satellites to observe weather and other phenomena that vary on short timescales.
38. Equinoxes
Equinoxes occur every year on the 20th or 21st of March and the 22nd or 23rd of September, and on these days all locations have the same duration of day and night. In the Northern Hemisphere, March Equinox is known as Vernal Equinox and Spring Equinox, while it is known as Autumnal Equinox and Fall Equinox in the Southern Hemisphere.
39. Moons of Inner Planets
Of the terrestrial (rocky) planets of the inner solar system, neither Mercury nor Venus have any moons at all; they are too close to the Sun. Any moon with too great a distance from these planets would be in an unstable orbit and be captured by the Sun.
40. Ursa Major
Ursa Major or Big Bear is a constellation in the Northern sky that is visible throughout the year from the Northern sky and appears circumpolar above the mid-northern latitudes. It can be seen during summertime in the early part of the night.
41. Jupiter
Jupiter is, by far, the largest planet in the solar system—more than twice as massive as all the other planets combined. Its mass is 318 times that of earth. Jupiter is the fifth planet from the Sun.
42. Steady State Theory
The steady-state theory was first put forward in 1948 by British scientists Sir Hermann Bondi, Thomas Gold, and Sir Fred Hoyle. It was further developed by Hoyle to deal with problems that had arisen in connection with the alternative big-bang hypothesis.
43. Nebular Hypothesis
The most widely accepted explanation of how the solar system formed is called the nebular hypothesis. According to this hypothesis, the Sun and the planets of our solar system formed about 4.6 billion years ago from the collapse of a giant cloud of gas and dust, called a nebula. The nebular hypothesis explains many of the features of the solar system like the orbital plane, the revolution and rotation of the planets, the relationship of the axes of rotation and the orbital plane, and the age of moon rocks.
44. Planetary Rings
Four of the planets in the Solar System have rings. They are the four giant gas planets: Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune. Mercury is the first planet from the Sun and the smallest in the Solar System; there are no rings around Mercury.
45. Nebula
A nebula is an interstellar cloud of dust, hydrogen, helium and other ionized gases. Some nebulae come from the gas and dust thrown out by the explosion of a dying star, such as a supernova. Other nebulae are regions where new stars are beginning to form.
46. Nearest Stars
The nearest star from Earth is the Sun. The second nearest star is Alpha Centauri or Proxima Centauri. Alpha Centauri is a triple star system in the constellation of Centaurus. It consists of Rigil Kentaurus, Toliman and Proxima Centauri. Proxima Centauri is the closest star to the Sun at 4.24 light years. It is 4.3 light years away from the Earth.
47. Mercury
Mercury is the first planet from the Sun and the smallest in the Solar System. It is the second densest planet, after Earth. It speeds around the Sun every 88 days, traveling through space nearly 29 miles (47 kilometers) per second, faster than any other planet. Mercury spins slowly on its axis and completes one rotation every 59 Earth days.

TYPE-II: Lithosphere & Earth Structure

1. Tectonic Plates
The largest tectonic plate on Earth is the Pacific plate, stretching almost 103,000,000 square kilometers. The smallest tectonic plate is the Juan de Fuca Plate, at approximately 250,000 square kilometers. The point within the earth where the actual movement takes place is called the focus. The point on the surface directly above the focus is called the epicenter.
2. Silicon
Silicon is the second most common element present in the crust after Oxygen. It exists as a compound in the mantle and the crust. In the crust, it exists combined with oxygen to form silicate minerals. It is found in the sand which is an abundant and easily accessible resource on Earth. Silicon is also recovered from quartzite, mica, and talc.
3. Equator
The geographic Equator divides the Earth into the Northern and Southern hemispheres and forms the imaginary reference line on the Earth's surface from which latitude is reckoned; in other words, it is the line with 0° latitude. The equator is the only line on the Earth's surface that is considered a great circle.
4. Earth's Temperature
The different observations show that the rate of increase of temperature is not uniform from the surface towards the earth's centre. In the beginning, this rate of increase of temperature is at an average rate of 1°C for every 32m increase in depth.
5. Earth's Core (NIFE)
The core of earth is composed mainly of iron (Fe) and nickel (Ni) and hence it is also called as NIFE. It is responsible for earth's magnetism. This layer has the maximum specific gravity. It lies between 2900 km and 6400 km below the earth's surface. It accounts for 16 percent of the earth's volume.
6. Circles of Latitude
On Earth, the Equator is an imaginary line on the surface, equidistant from the North and South Poles, dividing the Earth into Northern and Southern Hemispheres. The latitude of the Earth's equator is, by definition, 0 degrees of arc. The Equator is one of the five notable circles of latitude on Earth; the other four are the Arctic Circle, the Antarctic Circle, the Tropic of Cancer, and the Tropic of Capricorn.
7. Isthmus
The narrow strip of land that connects two extensive landmasses is called an isthmus. An isthmus either connects two continents or joins a peninsula with the mainland. Panama and Suez isthmus are the typical examples of an isthmus.
9. Latitude and Longitude
In geography, latitude refers to the horizontal lines that circle the globe. Meridians (lines running from the North Pole to the South Pole) connect points with the same longitude.
10. Prime Meridian
A prime meridian is a meridian in a geographic coordinate system at which longitude is defined to be 0°.
11. Time and Longitude
The earth has been divided into 360 imaginary longitudinal lines. Since 360 degrees divided by a 24-hour day results in 15 degrees per hour, 1 degree equals 4 minutes. So for a difference of two hours, the longitudinal difference will be equal to 30°.
12. Day and Night
Day and night is caused by the rotation of Earth on its axis. Earth takes about 24 hours to rotate around its axis once. When a place on Earth faces the sun, it has daytime. When it faces away from the sun, it has night.
13. Gore
The distance between two longitude lines is called a gore. A gore is the curved surface which lies between two close lines of longitude on a globe.
14. Earth's Layers (SIAL, SIMA, NIFE)
The Earth has three layers:
  • Outer layer: Continents (SIAL) - silica and alumina are the main mineral constituents.
  • Mantle and oceanic crust: SIMA - mainly consist of silica and magnesium. Typically, when the magma comes to the surface it is basalt, so sometimes this layer is called the 'basalt layer' of the crust.
  • Inner layer: Core (NIFE) - nickel and iron. Outer core is liquid and inner core is solid.
15. Equinox
The word equinox is derived from the Latin aequus (equal) and nox (night). It refers to the days when daytime and night are of approximately equal duration. An equinox occurs twice a year around 20 March and 22 September.
21. Petrology
Petrology refers to the scientific study of rocks and the conditions which influence their formation. Petrology is a branch of geology that focuses on the chemical analysis in various fields such as petrography and mineralogy. It is concerned with all three major types of rocks: Igneous, metamorphic, and sedimentary.
22. Gneiss
Gneiss is a common and widely distributed type of metamorphic rock. Gneiss is formed by high temperature and high-pressure metamorphic processes acting on formations composed of igneous or sedimentary rocks. Orthogneiss is gneiss derived from igneous rock; Paragneiss is gneiss derived from sedimentary rock.
23. Granite
Granite is a light-colored igneous rock that forms from the slow crystallization of magma below Earth's surface. Strictly speaking, granite is an igneous rock with between 20% and 60% quartz by volume, and at least 35% of the total feldspar consisting of alkali feldspar.
25. Dhaulagiri
Dhaulagiri mountain of Nepal is also known as White Mountain. The name of Dhaulagiri comes from Sanskrit where dhawala means dazzling white/beautiful and giri means mountain. Dhaulagiri is the seventh highest mountain in the world at 8,167 metres, and the highest mountain within the borders of a single country (Nepal).
26. Continental Crust (SIAL)
Continental crust is the outside layer of the earth and is made of solid rock, mostly basalt and granite. It is less dense, thicker, and mainly composed of granite. It is also called SIAL (the most abundant minerals being silica and aluminum).
27. Mount Kilimanjaro
Mount Kilimanjaro began to form nearly 750,000 years ago when molten lava erupted through the Earth's crust and began to push rock and sediment upwards. It is a stratovolcano and is made up of ash, lava, and rock, with large quantities of basalts and andesites.
28. Feldspar
Feldspar is the name of a large group of rock-forming silicate minerals that make up over 50% of Earth's crust. They are found in igneous, metamorphic, and sedimentary rocks in all parts of the world.
29. Asthenosphere
The asthenosphere is the ductile layer situated beneath Earth's rigid lithosphere. The enormous heat and pressure at these depths causes the material in the mantle to soften and become flexible. It behaves plastically and can flow like a liquid with a viscous caramel-like consistency.
30. Weathering
Weathering refers to the combined action of all processes that cause rock to disintegrate physically and decompose chemically because of exposure near the Earth's surface. Weathering also creates a number of distinctive landforms.
31. International Date Line (IDL)
The International Date Line (IDL) is an imaginary line that runs from the North to the South Pole and separates two calendar days. The IDL roughly follows the 180° line of longitude and passes through the Pacific Ocean.
32. Mount Everest
Mount Everest is also known in Nepal as Sagarmatha and in Tibet as Chomolungma. Sagarmatha literally means "Peak of Heaven". In the nineteenth century, the mountain was named after Sir George Everest, a former Surveyor General of India.
33. Lithospheric Plates
The lithosphere of Earth is broken into several irregularly shaped parts, called the plates or tectonic plates. The theory of plate tectonics proves that the earth's lithosphere is divided into 7 major and minor plates. There are seven major plates: African, Antarctic, Eurasian, Indo-Australian, North American, Pacific, and South American.
35. Earthquakes
Earthquakes are the result of sudden movement along faults within the Earth. The movement releases stored-up 'elastic strain' energy in the form of seismic waves, which propagate through the Earth and cause the ground surface to shake.
36. Nazca Plate
A tectonic plate is a massive, irregularly shaped slab of solid rock. Nazca is a minor plate between the South American and Pacific plates.
37. Badlands
The Badlands are a type of arid terrain with clay-rich soil that has been extensively eroded by wind and water. They are marked by extensive erosion of softer sedimentary rocks and clay-rich soils. Canyons, ravines, gullies, hoodoos and other such geological forms are common in the Badlands.
38. Mountain Range
A mountain range is a gathering or chain of mountains found near one another in a line. The Himalayas, the Alps and the Andes are mountain ranges of Asia, Europe and South America respectively. The Aravalli Range, an eroded stub of ancient mountains, is believed to be the oldest range of fold mountains in India.
40. Sheet Erosion
Sheet erosion, the uniform removal of soil in thin layers, is the least visible form of erosion. It occurs when soil particles are carried evenly over the soil surface by rainwater that does not infiltrate into the ground. Sheet erosion is least noticeable as it does not leave obvious cuts in the soil surface.
41. Landslides
Studies have revealed that rainfall, soil characteristics, and anthropogenic activities such as deforestation and overgrazing are the leading causes of landslides. Vegetation cover and vegetation root systems bind the soil on slopes and are effective means of reducing the rate of surface runoff, which practically reduces landslides.
42. Erosion vs Weathering
Erosion refers to the phenomenon of gradual wearing away of the landscape by natural agents like water, wind, and ice. It is the geological process in which earthen materials are worn away and transported by natural forces. A similar process, weathering, breaks down or dissolves rock, but does not involve movement.
44. Marble
Marble is a metamorphic rock that forms when limestone is subjected to the heat and pressure of metamorphism. It is composed primarily of the mineral calcite (CaCO3) and usually contains other minerals, such as clay minerals, micas, quartz, pyrite, iron oxides, and graphite. Under the conditions of metamorphism, the calcite in the limestone recrystallizes to form a rock that is a mass of interlocking calcite crystals.
45. Lithification
Lithification, a complex process whereby deposited loose grains of sediment are converted into rock, is common to all sedimentary rocks. Sedimentary rocks are formed at or near the Earth's surface by the accumulation and lithification of fragments of preexisting rocks or by precipitation from solution at normal surface temperatures.
46. Prayagraj (Triveni Sangam)
Prayagraj, formerly known as Allahabad, is located at the confluence of the Ganges (Ganga), the Yamuna, and the mythical Saraswati River. The meeting point is known as Triveni Sangam and is especially sacred to Hindus.
48. Earth's Shape
As Earth is flattened at the poles, the radius of the equator is greater than the radius of the poles. The radius of Earth at the poles (polar radius) is 6,356 km and rises to 6,378 km at the equator.
49. Harry Hammond Hess
Harry Hammond Hess is considered one of the 'founding fathers' of the unifying theory of plate tectonics. He published "The History of Ocean Basins" in which he outlined a theory that could explain how the continents could actually drift.
50. Continental Mass (SIAL)
The main mineral constituents of the continental mass are silica and alumina. It is for this very reason that is also called SIAL (si-silica and al-alumina).
51. Basalt
Basalt is an aphanitic (fine-grained) extrusive igneous rock. It is low in silica content, dark in color, and comparatively rich in iron and magnesium. It is formed from the rapid cooling of lava rich in magnesium and iron exposed at or near the surface of the earth.
58. Geomorphology
Geomorphology is the scientific discipline concerned with the description and classification of the earth's topographic features. Geomorphology studies the origin and evolution of topographic and bathymetric features created by physical, chemical, or biological processes operating at or near Earth's surface.
59. Potassium
Potassium (K) is the seventh most common element, accounting for around 2.6 percent of the Earth's crust. This highly reactive element is never found free in nature and produces a variety of compounds.
60. Chilika Lake
Chilika Lake is Asia's biggest inland saltwater lake and lagoon. It is spread over the Puri, Khurda, and Ganjam districts of Odisha state on the east coast of India, at the mouth of the Daya River. In 1981, Chilika Lake was designated the first Indian wetland of international importance under the Ramsar Convention.
61. Slate
Slate is a low-grade metamorphic rock. It is generally formed by the metamorphosis of mudstone/shale, or sometimes basalt, under relatively low pressure and temperature conditions.
62. Perihelion
Earth is nearest to the Sun in early January every year, and is said to be at perihelion. The term perihelion comes from the Greek words peri, meaning 'near' and helios, meaning 'sun'. The perihelion is the closest distance between Earth and the Sun. In early July, when it is summer in the Northern Hemisphere, Earth is farthest from the Sun, termed as aphelion.
63. Elements of Earth's Crust
  • Oxygen (O): 47%
  • Silicon (Si): 28%
  • Aluminum (Al): 8.0%
  • Iron (Fe): 5.0%
  • Calcium (Ca): 3.6%
  • Sodium (Na): 2.8%
  • Potassium (K): 2.6%
  • Magnesium (Mg): 2.1%
  • Titanium (Ti): 0.6%
  • Hydrogen(H): 0.1%
  • Other element: 0.5%
64. Alkaline Earth Metals
The correct sequence of alkaline earth metals, according to atomic number, is: Be (Beryllium, 4), Mg (Magnesium, 12), Ca (Calcium, 20), Sr (Strontium, 38), Ba (Barium, 56). Alkaline earth metals are a group of chemical elements that share similar properties. They are all shiny, silvery-white, somewhat reactive metals.
65. Sandstone and Limestone
Sandstone is a sedimentary rock composed of sand grains of mineral, rock, or organic material. It is formed when sand grains are cemented together by minerals such as silica, calcite, or hematite. Limestone is a sedimentary rock composed of calcium carbonate. It is formed when shells of marine organisms, such as clams and snails, accumulate on the ocean floor and are compacted and cemented together.
66. Age of Earth
Based on current scientific knowledge, the Earth is estimated to be about 4.54 billion years old. This estimate is based on a variety of dating methods, including radiometric dating, which measures the decay of radioactive isotopes to determine the age of rocks and minerals. The oldest rocks have been found to be about 4.4 billion years old.
67. Collapse Earthquake
Collapse earthquakes are small earthquakes in underground caverns and mines that are caused by seismic waves produced from the explosion of rock on the surface. A volcanic earthquake is caused by volcanic activity. An explosive earthquake is caused due to the testing of nuclear weapons. Tectonic earthquake is caused due to the movement of the slab of rocks/plates.
68. Esker
Esker is a long winding ridge of stratified sand and gravel associated with former glaciers. They are considered to be channel deposits that were let down onto the ground surface as the glacier retreated.
69. Geoid
The Earth is slightly flattened at the poles. That is why its shape is described as a Geoid. Geoid means an earth-like shape.
71. Metamorphism
Metamorphic rocks form when heat and pressure transform an existing rock into a new rock. The word metamorphism comes from ancient Greek words for "change" (meta) and "form" (morph). The type of rock that a metamorphic rock used to be prior to metamorphism is called the protolith.
72. Tsunami
Tsunami (a Japanese word) is a very large and powerful wave. It is caused by earthquakes under the sea. The effect of a tsunami would occur only if the epicentre of the tremor is below oceanic waters and the magnitude is sufficiently high.
73. Torrid Zone
The Torrid zone is a region between the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn. This region receives the direct vertical rays of the Sun for almost the whole year. Therefore, it gets the maximum heat from the Sun.

TYPE-III: Physical Geography & Climate

1. Winds (Chinook, Foehn, Loo)
  • Chinook: a warm, westerly wind of Canada and USA.
  • Foehn: a warm, dry, gusty wind of the Alps.
  • Sirocco: blows by the local name of Khamsin.
  • Loo: hot and dry summer afternoon wind that blows in Indo-Gangetic Plain region of North India.
  • Avalanche: refers to the fall or slide of a large mass of material, especially of snow, down a mountainside.
2. Eye of a Cyclone
The eye is a region of mostly calm weather at the center of strong cyclones. The eye of a storm is a roughly circular area, typically 30–65 km in diameter. It is surrounded by the eyewall, a ring of towering thunderstorms where the most severe weather and highest winds occur.
3. Weather vs Climate
The day-to-day condition of the atmosphere at a place with respect to the temperature, humidity, rainfall, windspeed, etc., is called the weather at that place. Weather describes the condition of the atmosphere over a short period of time (day to day or week to week), while climate describes average conditions over a longer period (30 years or more).
4. Tropical Cyclones Names
Tropical cyclones are known by various names in different parts of the world. In the North Atlantic Ocean and the eastern North Pacific, they are called hurricanes. In the western North Pacific around the Philippines, Japan, and China, they are referred to as typhoons.
5. Equatorial Rainfall
Equatorial regions receive rainfall throughout the year and thus there is no dry season. Average annual rainfall exceeds 200 cm to 250 cm. Winds from two directions—North-East Trade winds and South-East trade winds—converge near the equator at the Inter Tropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ), an area of low atmospheric pressure.
6. Land Breeze
Land breeze is a local wind system characterized by a flow from land to water late at night. Land breezes alternate with sea breezes along coastlines adjacent to large bodies of water. Both are induced by differences that occur between the heating and cooling of the water surface and the adjacent land surface.
7. Wind
The movement of air from one region to another creates wind. Movement of air is caused by temperature or pressure differences and is experienced as wind. Where there are differences in pressure between two places, a pressure gradient exists, across which air moves: from the high-pressure region to the low-pressure region.
8. Greenhouse Gases
A greenhouse gas is defined as any gaseous compound that is capable of absorbing infrared radiation, thereby trapping and holding heat in the atmosphere. Many greenhouse gases occur naturally in the atmosphere, such as carbon dioxide, methane, water vapor, and nitrous oxide. Synthetic ones include chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs), and Perfluoro-carbons (PFCs).
13. Atmosphere Composition
Earth's atmosphere is composed of about 78 percent nitrogen, 21 percent oxygen, 0.9 percent argon, and 0.1 percent other gases. Trace amounts of carbon dioxide, methane, water vapor, and neon are some of the other gases that make up the remaining 0.1 percent.
14. Köppen Climate Classification
German-Russian climatologist Wladimir Köppen published his climate classification in 1884. His climate classification divides climates into five main climate groups: A (tropical), B (dry), C (temperate), D (continental), and E (polar).
16. Death Valley
Death Valley is a desert valley in Eastern California. During summer, it is one of the hottest places on Earth. On 10 July 1913, Furnace Creek in Death Valley recorded a high temperature of 134 °F (56.7°C) which stands as the highest ambient air temperature ever recorded on the surface of the Earth.
17. ITCZ
The Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ) is a narrow belt of low pressure which circles the Earth generally near the equator where the trade winds of the Northern and Southern Hemispheres come together. It is characterised by convective activity which generates vigorous thunderstorms over large areas.
17. Köppen Codes
In the Köppen classification, the letter W stands for Desert type of climate, while the letter w stands for Dry winter climate.
18. Cold Climate with Dry Winters (Dw)
As per the Köppen climate classification, Cold Climate with Dry Winters (Dw) is mainly found over North Eastern Asia. Precipitation occurs in summer. The annual precipitation is low from 12-15 cm.
19. Tropical Savanna
Tropical savanna climate or tropical wet and dry climate corresponds to the Köppen climate classification categories Aw (for a dry winter) and As (for a dry summer). Tropical savanna climates are most commonly found in Africa, Asia, and South America.
20. El Niño
El Niño is a climate pattern that describes the unusual warming of surface waters in the eastern tropical Pacific Ocean. It is the "warm phase" of a larger phenomenon called the El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO). The El Niño event is not a regular cycle; they are not predictable and occur irregularly at two to seven year intervals.
21. Marine Climate (Cfb)
Cfb is an example of marine climate that is found on the western edges or coasts of the continents. Here, hot and dry summers can be found with largely humid conditions, whereas winters are mild with heavy precipitation.
22. Dendrochronology
Dendrochronology is the scientific discipline concerned with dating and interpreting past events, particularly paleoclimates and climatic trends, based on the analysis of tree rings. It is also called tree-ring dating.
23. Dew Point
Dew Point Temperature is defined as the temperature at which air would have to cool (at constant pressure and constant water vapor content) in order to reach saturation. The dew point gives insight into the amount of moisture in the air. The higher the dew point temperature, the higher the moisture content.
24. Ozone Hole
The term ozone hole refers to the depletion of the protective ozone layer in the upper atmosphere (stratosphere) over Earth's polar regions. About 90% of Earth's ozone resides in the stratosphere. The ozone layer is very important for life on Earth because it absorbs the most damaging form of UV radiation.
25. Thermosphere
The thermosphere is a layer of Earth's atmosphere that is directly above the mesosphere and below the exosphere. It lies above the mesopause, and is a region in which temperatures again increase with height. This temperature increase is caused by the absorption of energetic ultraviolet and X-Ray radiation from the Sun.
28. Polar Easterlies
Polar easterlies are dry and cold permanent winds that blow from north-east to south-west direction in the Northern Hemisphere and south-east to north-west in the Southern Hemisphere. They emanate from the polar highs, areas of high pressure around the North and South Poles.
29. Jet Streams
Jet Streams are high velocity winds that blow in the upper troposphere at an altitude of around 12,000 metres. These winds are faster than surface winds as there are no obstacles in the upper troposphere. The speed can vary from 110 to 184 km/h in winter.
32. Global Warming
Global warming refers to the general increase in the earth's average temperature. The burning of fossil fuels and deforestation causes the temperature of the earth to increase. High temperature changes the weather patterns, causing dry areas to get drier and wet areas to get wetter.
33. Troposphere
The troposphere extends from the surface to about 18 km near the equator and up to 10 km at the poles. Greenhouse gases accumulate in the lower layer of the troposphere.
35. El Niño and La Niña
El Niño and La Niña are opposing phases of the El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO). In Spanish, El Niño means "small boy" or "Christ child". El Niño during winter causes warm conditions over the Indian subcontinent and during summer leads to dry conditions and deficient monsoon rainfall. La Niña results in better than normal monsoon in India.
36. Convectional Rainfall
Convectional rainfall occurs when the heated air from the earth's surface rises upwards along with the water vapour and gets condensed when it reaches a higher altitude. Convectional rainfall is common in the equatorial calm belt.
41. Mawsynram
Mawsynram, located on the crest of Khasi hills in Meghalaya, receives the highest average annual rainfall in the world. It receives the majority of its rainfall from the Bay of Bengal branch of the Indian monsoon. As it is located on the southern slopes of Khasi hills, it is bordered by hills on three sides.
42. Ozone Layer
The ozone layer is contained in the stratosphere. The stratosphere is the second layer of the Earth's atmosphere, extending from about 10 to 50 kilometers above the Earth's surface. The ozone layer acts as a filter and absorbs the ultra-violet rays radiating from the sun.
45. General Circulation
The pattern of the movement of the planetary winds is called general circulation of the atmosphere. Three cells set the pattern for the general circulation: Hadley cell, Ferrel cell and Polar cell.
46. Fusion vs Freezing
The physical process that results in transition from liquid to solid state is known as freezing or solidification. Fusion is also known as melting point.
47. Biogeochemical Cycles
The cyclic movements of chemical elements of the biosphere between the organism and the environment are referred to as biogeochemical cycles. There are two types: the gaseous (atmosphere reservoir) and the sedimentary cycle (soil/rock reservoir).
50. Milankovitch Cycles
Milankovitch cycles describe the collective effects of changes within the Earth's movements on its climate over thousands of years. They are named after Serbian geophysicist Milutin Milankovic. These cycles provide a strong framework for understanding long-term changes in Earth's climate, including the beginning and end of Ice Ages.
52. Orographic Rainfall
Orographic rainfall occurs when a mass of air is pushed up because of a structure with very high elevation, such as a mountain. The Western Ghats in India provide favorable conditions for orographic rainfall.
53. Cyclones
The word Cyclone is derived from the Greek word Cyclos meaning the coils of a snake. Tropical cyclones develop in tropical oceans at least 5-30° latitude north or south of the equator, where the sea temperature is at least 27°C.
54. Waterspouts
Waterspouts are significant atmospheric phenomena characterised by rotating columns of air that form over water bodies. It is a weaker version of a tornado, typically lasting 5-10 minutes.

TYPE-IV: Oceanography & Hydrosphere

1. Terrigenous Sediments
In oceanography, terrigenous sediments are those derived from the erosion of rocks on land. Consisting of sand, mud, and silt carried to sea by rivers, they are mainly deposited on the continental shelf and slopes.
2. Continental Shelf
A continental shelf extends from the coastline of a continent to a drop-off point called the shelf break. From the break, the shelf descends toward the deep ocean floor in what is called the continental slope. Even though they are underwater, continental shelves are part of the continent.
4. Red Sea
The Red Sea is a seawater inlet of the Indian Ocean, lying between Africa and Asia. The connection to the ocean is in the south through the Bab el Mandeb strait and the Gulf of Aden.
5. Archipelago
An archipelago is a group of islands closely scattered in a body of water. Most archipelagos are made of oceanic islands. An archipelago of oceanic islands is called an island arc.
6. Strait of Gibraltar
The Strait of Gibraltar is a narrow strait that connects the Atlantic Ocean to the Mediterranean Sea and separates Gibraltar and Peninsular Spain in Europe from Morocco in Africa.
7. Ocean Currents
Ocean currents consist of horizontal and vertical components of the circulation system of ocean waters that is produced by gravity, wind friction, and water density variation. Warm ocean currents originate near the equator and move towards the poles. Cold currents originate near the poles and move towards the tropics.
  • Gulf Stream: Warm Current
  • Agulhas Current: Warm Current
  • Labrador Current: Cold Current
  • Alaskan Current: Warm Current
8. Tides
The rhythmic rise and fall of ocean water twice a day is known as a tide. Tides are caused by the strong gravitational pull exerted by the sun and the moon on the earth's surface.
10. OTEC
OTEC stands for Ocean Thermal Energy Conversion. It is a process that can produce electricity by using the temperature difference between deep cold ocean water and warm tropical surface waters.
14. Ring of Fire
The Ring of Fire, also referred to as the Circum-Pacific Belt, is a path along the Pacific Ocean characterized by active volcanoes and frequent earthquakes. Seventy-five percent of Earth's volcanoes (more than 450 volcanoes) are located along the Ring of Fire.
15. Arctic Ocean
The Arctic Ocean is the smallest and shallowest of the world's five major oceans. The Arctic Ocean makes up only 4.3 percent of the global ocean. Roughly 46.6 percent of the global ocean, experts consider the Pacific to be the world's largest ocean.
16. Siberian Shelf
The Siberian Shelf is the largest continental shelf of the Earth. It is a part of the continental shelf of Russia and extends from the continent of Eurasia into the Arctic Ocean. It stretches to 1,500 kilometers offshore.
17. Southern Ocean
The Southern Ocean has been recognised by the US Board on Geographic Names as the body of water extending from the coast of Antarctica to the line of latitude at 60 degrees South. It is the fourth largest water body in the world.
18. Arabian Sea
The Arabian Sea is in the northwestern part of the Indian Ocean. It is bounded to the west by the Horn of Africa and the Arabian Peninsula, to the north by Iran and Pakistan, to the east by India.
19. Salinity
The salinity of water in the surface layer of oceans depends mainly on evaporation and precipitation. Surface salinity is greatly influenced in coastal regions by the freshwater flow from rivers, and in polar regions by the processes of freezing and thawing of ice.
21. Indian Ocean
The Indian Ocean basin is the world's third-largest ocean with an area of about 70,560,000 square km. It covers almost 20% of the Earth's surface.
22. Deep Sea Plains
Deep sea plains are gently sloping areas of the ocean basins. These are the flattest and smoothest regions of the world because of terrigenous and shallow water sediments that bury the irregular topography.
24. Gulf of Mannar
Gulf of Mannar is a large shallow bay forming part of the Laccadive Sea in the Indian Ocean that is situated between the west coast of Sri Lanka and the southeastern tip of India. The Adam's Bridge separates the Gulf of Mannar from Palk Bay.
27. Atolls
Atolls are low islands found in the tropical oceans consisting of coral reefs surrounding a central depression (lagoon).
30. Oceanic vs Continental Crust
The oceanic crust (land under oceans) is 5 to 10 kms thick and is mainly composed of silica and magnesium (SIMA). The continental crust is 10 to 30 kms thick, and it is mainly composed of silica and alumina (SIAL).
32. Seafloor Spreading
Seafloor spreading is a geologic process in which tectonic plates (large slabs of Earth's lithosphere) split apart from each other. Seafloor spreading occurs along mid-ocean ridges. The Mid-Atlantic Ridge is formed in the Atlantic Ocean by seafloor spreading.
34. Pearl Oyster
Pinctada margaritifera is commonly known as the Black-Lipped Pearl Oyster. It is a marine bivalve mollusk of the family Pteriidae.
35. Lake Van
Lake Van in Turkey is the largest soda or alkaline lake on Earth. It is an endorheic lake (it has no outlet), so its water disappears by evaporation, leading to high salinity levels. Lake Van has the highest salinity in the world at 330‰. Next is the Dead Sea (240‰) and the Great Salt Lake (220‰).

TYPE-V: Resources & Agriculture

1. Slash-and-burn
Slash-and-burn agriculture is a farming method that involves the cutting and burning of plants in a forest or woodland to create a field. The ash provides some fertilization, and the plot is relatively free of weeds. It is often used by tropical rainforest farmers in various parts of the world.
2. Bauxite
Australia is the world's largest producer of Bauxite, followed by China and Guinea. Bauxite is the principal ore of aluminum. Bauxite is associated with laterite rocks.
3. Lignite
Lignite coal, aka brown coal, is the lowest grade coal with the least concentration of carbon. Lignite has a low heating value and a high moisture content and is mainly used in electricity generation.
4. Mixed Farming
Mixed Farming is the land used for growing food and fodder crops and rearing livestock.
5. Plantations
Major plantations are found in the tropical regions of the world. Rubber in Malaysia, coffee in Brazil, tea in India and Sri Lanka are some examples.
6. Humification
Humification occurs during decomposition. Humus is the dark organic matter that forms in soil when dead plant and animal matter breaks down. The humification process in soil takes place through weathering and decomposition.

TYPE-VI: Ecology & Environment

1. Ecosystem
An ecosystem consists of a community of organisms (different species living together) and their physical environment (soil, water, and weather).
2. Endemism
Endemism is the ecological state of a species being unique to a defined geographic location, such as an island, nation, country or other defined zone. Species found in such restricted geographical areas are called endemic species.
3. Oil Spill
An oil spill is the release of a liquid petroleum hydrocarbon into the environment, especially the marine ecosystem, due to human activity.
4. Ecological Pyramid
An ecological pyramid is a graphical representation designed to show the biomass or bio-productivity at each trophic level in a given ecosystem.
5. Inverted Biomass Pyramid
Marine ecosystems have inverted biomass pyramids because the producers are tiny phytoplankton that grow and reproduce rapidly. Here, the pyramid of biomass has a small base, with the consumer biomass at any instant actually exceeding the producer biomass.
6. Grassland Pyramid
In a grassland ecosystem, the number of producers (mainly grasses) is always maximum, followed by decreasing numbers at the second trophic level (herbivores) and third trophic level (carnivores). Thus, a pyramid of number in grassland is upright.
9. Biosphere
The narrow zone of contact between the lithosphere, hydrosphere and atmosphere is called the biosphere. It is in this zone that life exists and flourishes.
10. Temperate Deciduous Forests
Temperate deciduous forests are located in the mid-latitude areas. These forests are dominated by broadleaved trees which lose their leaves during winter.
14. Carrying Capacity
Carrying capacity refers to the maximum abundance of a species that can be sustained within a given habitat. When a biological population is in equilibrium with the carrying capacity of its environment, the birth and death rates are equal.
15. Temperate Evergreen Forests
Temperate evergreen forests are found in mid-latitudinal coastal regions. They lie on the eastern coastal margins of the continents (e.g., SE USA, South China).
16. Productivity
In ecology, productivity is the rate at which energy is added to the bodies of organisms in the form of biomass. Gross primary productivity is the total amount of biomass produced by producers. Net primary productivity is the assimilated biomass that can be passed on to the next trophic level (GPP minus respiration).
17. Ecosystem Terminology
The term Ecosystem was coined by British ecologist Arthur Tansley in 1935.
20. Father of Modern Ecology
Eugene P. Odum, an American biologist at the University of Georgia, is widely considered as the father of modern ecology.
25. Deodar
Deodar (Cedrus deodara) is a large evergreen coniferous tree that is found in mountains. It is a species of cedar native to the Himalayan Mountains. The name translates from Sanskrit as "timber of the gods".
26. Trophic Levels
The position in a food web or chain is known as a trophic level.
  • First level: Producers
  • Second level: Primary consumers
  • Third level: Secondary consumers
  • Fourth level: Tertiary consumers
28. Artificial Ecosystem
The ecosystem made by humans where both living and non-living components interact is called an artificial ecosystem (e.g., Aquarium, Garden).
30. Moist Deciduous Forests
Moist deciduous forests are found in areas receiving rainfall between 200 and 100 cm. Teak is the most dominant species of this forest.
31. Autecology
Autecology is the study of the relationships between individual organisms or species and their environment. Synecology is the study of ecology at the level of communities.
32. Mangroves
Mangrove tidal forests are found in the areas of coasts influenced by tides. Mud and silt accumulate on such coasts. Dense mangroves are the common varieties with roots of the plants submerged under water. The deltas of the Ganga, the Mahanadi, the Krishna, the Godavari and the Kaveri are covered by such vegetation.
34. Tropical Evergreen Forests
Tropical Evergreen forests are found in the western slopes of the Western Ghats, hills of the northeastern region and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands. They are found in warm and humid areas with annual precipitation of over 200 cm. There is no definite time for trees to shed leaves; as such, these forests appear green all the year round. Species include ebony, mahogany, rosewood, etc.

TYPE-VII: World Geography & Features

3. Stromboli
Stromboli is a small island in the Tyrrhenian Sea, off the north coast of Sicily. It is one of the most active volcanoes in the world. It is called the "Lighthouse of the Mediterranean Sea" because of its continuous eruptions.
5. Great Dividing Range
The Great Dividing Range, or the Eastern Highlands, is Australia's most substantial mountain range and the third longest land-based range in the world. It stretches more than 3,500 kilometers.
7. Lake Victoria
Lake Victoria lies on a plateau between the Western and Eastern Rift Valleys. Most of the lake lies south of the equator. It is the largest lake in Africa and the chief reservoir of the Nile.
